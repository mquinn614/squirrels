Central Park · October 2018

Three Thousand Squirrels

Over eleven days in the fall of 2018, hundreds of volunteers walked every hectare of Central Park to count every squirrel they could find. They tallied 3,023 sightings, noting what each squirrel was doing, what it looked like, what it said, and how it felt about the people watching it.

The count

Mostly gray, sometimes not

Nearly every squirrel here is an eastern gray: 0 of them. But 392 cinnamon and 103 black squirrels are color variants of the very same species.

They are not spread evenly. Cinnamon squirrels lean south; nearly two-thirds were spotted below the park's midline. The black ones cluster north, in the deep woods. If you want to see one, head for the North Woods and look up.

The park speaks

Volunteers logged three kinds of calls. A kuk (0 heard) is a sharp warning bark, often aimed at a hawk or an off-leash dog. A quaa (50) is a longer, descending alarm. The rare moan (3) signals a threat still far away.

The north end is the loud end: two-thirds of all kuks came from the upper half of the park.

Friend, foe, or furniture

For every sighting, volunteers noted the squirrel's reaction to them. 0 were indifferent. 678 ran, mostly in the wilder north. And 178 approached, clustering toward the southern end, where the tourists and their snacks are.

Still out there

The census counted 3,023 squirrels. The squirrels did not count back. They buried their acorns, teased the dogs, watched the counters come and go. And they're all still there, gray and cinnamon and black, breathing in the trees between Fifth Avenue and Central Park West.

If you go: October. Early morning. Stand near the oaks and bring nothing. They'll find you anyway.

About the data. The 2018 Central Park Squirrel Census was organized by The Squirrel Census, a small group of researchers and volunteers who fanned out across the park to count, observe, and record every squirrel they saw. Their dataset (3,023 sightings, with fur colors, activities, calls, and verbatim field notes) is published on NYC OpenData. All quotes are from the volunteers' actual notes.
